# Writing Craft

## Structure

### The Arc (Universal)
Every piece of writing — article, essay, story, email — has a shape:
1. **Hook** — grab attention, create a question or tension
2. **Context** — give the reader what they need to follow
3. **Development** — build your argument, story, or explanation
4. **Climax/Key Point** — the thing everything has been building toward
5. **Resolution** — land it. What should the reader take away?

### Paragraph Structure
- **One idea per paragraph** — if you're making two points, split them
- **Topic sentence first** (usually) — tell them what this paragraph is about
- **Support** — evidence, examples, elaboration
- **Transition** — connect to the next paragraph

### Transitions
The glue between ideas. Weak transitions = disjointed writing.

**Techniques:**
- Echo a word or concept from the previous paragraph
- Use transitional phrases sparingly (however, therefore, meanwhile)
- Ask a question that the next section answers
- Use a one-sentence paragraph as a bridge

## Voice and Style

### Finding Your Voice
Voice isn't something you construct — it emerges when you stop trying to sound like someone else.

**Voice elements:**
- **Sentence length** — Short and punchy? Long and flowing? Mixed?
- **Vocabulary** — Simple and direct? Rich and layered? Technical?
- **Tone** — Serious? Playful? Conversational? Authoritative?
- **Rhythm** — How do your sentences sound read aloud?
- **Perspective** — How close are you to the reader? Formal distance or intimate?

### Style Guidelines
- **Active voice** over passive (usually): "The team built the product" > "The product was built by the team"
- **Strong verbs** over adverb+weak verb: "She sprinted" > "She ran quickly"
- **Concrete** over abstract: "Revenue dropped 30%" > "Results were disappointing"
- **Specific** over vague: "Three customers complained about load times" > "Some users had issues"
- **Vary sentence length** — monotonous rhythm puts readers to sleep

### Clarity
- If a sentence requires re-reading, rewrite it
- Remove words that don't add meaning
- One idea per sentence (complex ideas get their own sentence)
- Avoid jargon unless your audience expects it
- When in doubt, simpler is better

## Common Weaknesses

### The Throat-Clearing Opener
Starting with context nobody needs before getting to the point.
- **Fix:** Delete the first paragraph. Your real opening is probably the second one.

### The Passive Hedge
"It could be argued that there might be some evidence to suggest..."
- **Fix:** Just say the thing. "The evidence suggests..."

### The Run-On
Cramming too many ideas into one sentence.
- **Fix:** Period. New sentence. Let the reader breathe.

### The Buried Lede
The most interesting thing is hidden in paragraph four.
- **Fix:** Move it to the top. Or at least tease it in the opening.

### The Weak Ending
Trailing off instead of landing.
- **Fix:** End with your strongest sentence. Restate the key insight or leave them with something to think about.

### Over-Qualification
"In my opinion, I think that perhaps..."
- **Fix:** State your point. The reader knows it's your opinion — you're the one writing.

## The Editing Mindset

Writing and editing are different modes. Don't do both at once.

**First draft:** Get ideas down. Don't judge. Don't polish. Just write.
**Second draft:** Fix structure. Move things around. Cut what doesn't serve the piece.
**Third draft:** Improve sentences. Tighten language. Strengthen verbs.
**Final pass:** Proofread. Fix errors. Read aloud.

**The golden rule of editing:** If you can remove a word without losing meaning, remove it.
